I really enjoyed A Link Between Worlds. It's fun, it's not too long, and it has some moments of real genius. It offers two very unique primary mechanics to the game, one of which is very good and makes me wish the other was better. The two mechanics, for those who don't know, are Link's ability to merge with walls and Ravio's shop. 

Let's start with the worse one, Ravio's rental system. It's not all bad, it does a good job in making rupees feel actually valuable, something that isn't really the case in a lot of Zelda games. It also lets you tackle the challenges in mostly whatever order you'd like. You can pick the thing you want and go to town. Both of these are strong positives.

The downside of that freedom is that the game can't count on you having anything. Instead of layering complexity, the game stays pretty flat. Problems never really require an accumulation of your items because they can't count on you having it because maybe you didn't buy it, or you did, you died, and you lost it. This means even if you cleared a dungeon that required the use of an item the game can't expect you to have it. The challenges need to be something where even a pretty mediocre player can advance through the game one rented item at a time. 

![[Pasted image 20261029163836.png]]

*This money grubbing jerk took my house.*

Another problem is that the game has too many items. I struggled to find a reason to use the boomerang. I had more pressing things to buy, things that would actually advance the game, and even when I had it, I had already permanently purchased the bow which was superior in most circumstances. 

Something that could have solved this would have been more flexibility with the rental and purchasing system. Just make the boomerang cheaper than the bow. Now the player has a choice of yes, this is worse than the bow, but it's so cheap and I can have it forever! Or, just give the player the boomerang for free as a baseline item. That might make the bow less of a priority since you'll always have *something.*

![[Pasted image 20261029164346.png]]

*Just farmin' rupees.*

As I said, this does make rupees more valuable, but almost too valuable in a way. Because I need so many rupees to get all my equipment, I really want to save and hoard them so I can buy instead of renting and losing the rupees. Some of this could be solved by having Ravio put aside some or all of the money used on rentals and put them towards the eventual purchase. This would encourage players to rent more things and expand their capabilities faster, instead of feeling like hoarding is the best path forward. Without that though, this is something of the Hylian version of the Vimes rule: if you're rich, you save more money which lets you buy more items. Unless you're very good at not dying, this is the best strategy. 

![[Pasted image 20261030225957.png]]

*Both fun to find and a good reward.*

This is also compounded by another mechanic I really liked and had fun with, the Maimais. I found hunting for them really fun and the rewards felt strong and meaningful. The problem is you can't upgrade rented weapons. This puts the emphasis further on slowly building out your arsenal with permanent weapons so you can then really benefit from those locked in Maimai powers.

Which kind of leads me to why even bother? The rental system is a cool idea but the incentives don't line up for it to be something you want to interact with. It very quickly became just a buy system for me. 

There is a hero mode that makes the game harder, but that doesn't address my problem which is that the puzzles and solutions remain flat. Making the execution barrier harder doesn't help that the game doesn't feel like it builds to anything mechanically. This is so evident in the final dungeon, this is where you'd expect a Zelda game to make you put it all together, to use all the tools you've earned and feel really clever and rewarded for having collected them all. Not having that feels like a let down. 

![[Pasted image 20261029163931.png]]

*This is the good mechanic.*

This is why the wall merge power stands out so strongly. It's available the entire game and that makes it something that offers a *second dimension* of problem solving: there's the traditional Zelda layer that's immediately apparent to you, and then there's the layer that you see when you start thinking about how to leverage that special power. It's reminiscent of the portal gun from Portal, a movement ability that encourages you to look at the world differently (now you're thinking with wall merges) and makes you feel brilliant (or maybe a little dumb) when you figure it out. Each item makes you feel like this at times, but mostly only in the one mandatory dungeon and then never again. 

![[Pasted image 20261029164204.webp]]

*I think we're too quick to forgive Hilda for her crimes.*

This is mostly an extended nitpick and analysis of this element of design that I thought had a bit too much friction. I really enjoyed A Link Between Worlds, it's definitely one of the best 2D Zeldas and really deserves a remake so people can experience it. The world and story are mostly fun and cute and it doesn't take too much of your time. There is a ton of creativity in this game and the base gameplay of Zelda is tried and true. As is, I would easily recommend it to basically anyone who likes Zelda (provided they had the specific hardware to run a 3DS game).
