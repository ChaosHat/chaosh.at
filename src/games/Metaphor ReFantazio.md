This is a game I really liked for the vast majority of the runtime. Let's start mechanical. I really love the systems of this game compared to Persona. From top to bottom they've made everything faster and more immediately legible. 

The class system is an excellent example: Persona's velvet room felt unknowable, I just threw things into the guillotine blender and assumed it was better because numbers? Archetypes are more traditionally understandable and because of that class builds are just fun. If there's a complaint here it's that the royal archetypes are too obviously good and their requirements too harsh, you have to devote most of your build for that and it's easy to have made mistakes for characters with really tight allocations (Heismay).

The social game is similarly improved. Metaphor's daily cadence is much more predictable than Persona and is more straightforward and less stressful with fewer pitfalls. Persona had a bunch of activities that were just wastes of time in hindsight and nothing warns you and Metaphor streamlines it to the things that matter. This also made it feel much more attainable to do everything, I was able to get everything I needed with days to spare. 

The turn based combat is so much more engaging because of the class system too, each party member is able to bring things to bear for a combat instead of being largely static (Sorry Ann, this dude is fire immune grab some bench). Being able to restart combats also removed having to waste a turn prodding an opponent for weaknesses, you figured it out and then got that time back. One could argue at this point we could have a tattle like in Thousand Year Door, but I'll take moves in the right direction.

Adding overworld combat was a big win, being able to deal with the trash quickly is a huge quality of life improvement and when you leveled enough to do it mid dungeon it felt like a milestone. It also added a different kind of mastery to work on with good benefits for doing so. Learning a mob's patterns, dodging out of the way and getting that opening round stun felt earned. The stun was also so valuable sometimes I'd flee the combat to get another chance at it. 

We're going to talk about story and characters now, spoilers abound below. 

I really liked the cast of characters here. They felt like comrades in arms around a shared goal compared to Persona's characters who don't seem to know what they want. Now, obviously some of that is due to where those characters are. Persona characters are high schoolers, they're figuring themselves out. All the same, I don't want to be hanging out with high schoolers. 

I was also surprised that there was no romance in this game, but the game is *so much better* for it. Persona romances felt shoehorned in there and there was no way to avoid it. Every female character fell in love with you and you just had to break their hearts. 

Let's move on to the plot itself. I really liked this story for the first 80% or so and was really excited to see where it goes...until it went. 

The game really wants to tell some kind of story where anyone can make a difference. You can be nothing, a small unknown Elda boy, an orphaned mixed race girl who lives in a cafe, a former noble who had everything taken away from them by tragedy, and armed with hope and the ability to convey a dream you can be the change. And then it loses all of that the instant it makes the main character the prince. 

Nowhere is this more obvious or tragic than Maria's story. She starts as a character in a similar spot to the main character and can look at him as a role model. The main character says "I am nothing and nobody respects me, but I'm doing my best to make a difference and you should too." She believes this, she does the best she can given she's a little girl. And then the prince revelation says "Sorry Maria, all your dreams are nice, but you need someone with power and legitimacy to actually do it." We get the little kid at the ending of The Last Jedi and then the Rey Palpatine reveal, immediately undercutting the first message. 

I'm sympathetic to the argument that I'm missing it, that the real difference maker isn't a person, but Utopia. While the main character drives the action they're just a messenger, a vessel for the story, a way for it to work in the real world. It's the story that rallies people to fight in order to make that dream real, or at least as close as humanly possible. The power of narrative is the true force!

And then More turns out to be the king. So even the dreams have to come from the right person. It's elite inspiring elites and we need to wait for our benevolent leaders to show us the right way. It's an odd flavor of great man theory. Maybe their defense is that the game shows the power of democracy and therefore believes in it, but somehow being forced between these options, the Prince and Louis, is almost worse, the status quo that promises this time will be different or burning it all down (put like that I guess it mirrors reality). It's also not like your character makes enduring democracy a part of their platform, the game really treats this as a one time monarchical exception. 

A particularly painful instance of this is that I loved Louis as a villain because he seemed so realistic. A charismatic strongman who pulls people into his orbit with his promises of equality. We see how desperate people would accept him as a savior and his offer to totally upend the system. The current game is rigged against the people, so flipping the table at least evens the odds even if it's not a guarantee of a better life. We have countless current real examples of how this can work and the power of this specific narrative. 

We as the players (through the characters) know this is a bad bargain, his ideal world where the strong prevail and everyone pulls themselves up by their own bootstraps is not better, in fact it's calamitous and leads to undue suffering. Some of our best characters realize this (Del and Basilio) and come to our side. 

And then Louis turns out to be a three headed demonic monster who literally eats people for power in his quest to see it all burn, not just the systems but the people. Everyone deserves to die, they're all (literally) monsters on the inside anyways. This lets Louis's ideology off the hook, maybe his ideas were fine, any policy goals would be bad and compromised if Dracula was espousing them!

The plot definitely has interesting things to say: the concept of magic being a power generated by your anxieties that can be wielded by people looking to rule you felt incredibly relevant to *gestures at everything happening*. It pokes at concepts of racism and democracy and the power of story that I think are legitimately cool and worth thinking about. The bones of something truly great are here, I just wish the game had the conviction to actually land it instead of weakening the message by choosing twists and spectacle at the Elda Village and Skybound Avatar. It's another Atlus game with this pattern and I'm not sure if they don't understand that or they're selling out and pulling punches for corpo overlords. It's disappointing to get this close and just back away from it at the end. 

Also when you first go to the Skybound Avatar I spent a bunch of consumables getting ready to fight Louis and then the fucker doesn't even fight you and I wasted all of that and that's basically unforgivable.
